\section{Introduction}

Universities spend a great deal of effort turning research into patents, licenses, and new companies. The rules for that work sit in each university's intellectual-property (IP) policy. The policy says who owns an invention, what faculty must disclose, how income is shared, how conflicts of interest are handled, and what inventors can expect from the technology-transfer office (TTO). Universities do not leave these documents alone forever; from time to time, often after many years, they rewrite them in response to new law, new governance priorities, or lessons learned in managing commercialization. That raises a simple question. What happens to technology-transfer outcomes when a university rewrites its IP policy?

The literature answers only part of it. The clearest within-system evidence comes from large changes in who owns inventions. When Norway abolished the ``professor's privilege'' and moved ownership from researchers to institutions, startups and patenting by university researchers fell sharply \citep{HvideJones2018}. Cross-institution studies link particular contract terms, especially inventor royalty shares, to commercialization \citep{LachSchankerman2008,ArqueCastellsEtAl2016}, but those links weaken once the policy data are corrected and expanded \citep{OuelletteTutt2020}. Research on TTOs looks elsewhere, at staffing, routines, incentives, and relationships with firms \citep{SiegelWaldmanLink2003,MarkmanEtAl2005,DebackereVeugelers2005}. What is missing is evidence on the ordinary revisions universities actually make. These rewrites usually keep institutional ownership in place while changing several provisions, and the language around them, at the same time. We know of no study that follows such rewrites as dated events and asks what happens to technology-transfer outcomes afterward. The companion paper that built our measure of policy language describes how that language varies across universities and over time, but it does not link it to outcomes \citep{SharmaPCSI2026}.

This paper studies those ordinary revisions. To classify which way a revision moves, we use the Policy Communication Stance Index (PCSI), developed and validated in \cite{SharmaPCSI2026}, which measures the communicative stance of formal policy text: whether a policy presents rights, obligations, authority, and assistance in supportive or restrictive terms. As in that paper, PCSI describes the text itself. It does not measure the legal generosity of the rules, how faculty perceive them, or how the technology-transfer office operates in practice. Most annual policy-in-force scores are simply carried forward from older documents, so we do not rely on them. Instead, we focus on the moments when one observed policy document replaces another. A revision that raises PCSI is an \emph{upward} revision; one that lowers it is a \emph{downward} revision. We then ask whether technology-transfer outcomes evolve differently around these two kinds of revision, once we account for what is happening at universities that did not revise.

Why would the stance of a written policy have anything to do with commercialization? Earlier work gives several reasons. Whether faculty enter the formal process depends partly on how costly they expect dealings with the TTO to be, and on the norms of the people around them \citep{OwenSmithPowell2001,BercovitzFeldman2008}. The TTO is an intermediary with its own objectives, routines, and incentives, and these shape what is disclosed, evaluated, licensed, and spun out \citep{JensenThursbyThursby2003,SiegelWaldmanLink2003,DebackereVeugelers2005,MarkmanEtAl2005}. Many university inventions are licensed at an early stage, so commercialization also depends on inventors, the office, and licensees continuing to work together after a deal is signed \citep{JensenThursby2001}. A formal policy can enter at each of these points. Inventors may read it, but it also sets the procedures, guidance, and lines of authority through which the office deals with them. Written procedures can impose real learning and compliance costs \citep{MoynihanHerdHarvey2015}, yet formal rules can also be partly ceremonial and drift away from practice \citep{MeyerRowan1977}, so a rewrite may simply mark a broader change inside the organization. These links also tell us where to look. If a policy works mainly through faculty, disclosures should respond; if it works through the office's dealings after disclosure, licensing should.

Building the sample carefully is a central part of the paper, so we do it in stages. We start with every pair of consecutive policy documents at the same university and call it a revision when the PCSI score changes by more than 0.03, about half its standard deviation. Among universities that report to AUTM, this gives 126 revisions at 78 universities. A mechanical screen then drops any revision that overlaps with another revision at the same university within the event window, or that has no outcome data before or after the event; 62 revisions survive. Next, we read the old and new documents for each of these 62. Twenty-five of these pairs had been reviewed in an earlier version of the paper, and we reviewed the remaining 37 under the same rules. We keep a revision only if the two documents are the same policy, or close enough to compare, and if we can date the change with reasonable confidence. Thirty-four revisions at 32 universities pass and form the preferred sample. A stricter version, which also requires at least one full year of outcomes after the revision, keeps 29. None of these rules uses the values of licensing or patenting outcomes, and every eligible pair is reviewed under the same criteria, so events are not selected on the basis of their estimated effects.

The main design is a stacked event study \citep{CengizEtAl2019,BakerLarckerWang2022}. Each revision gets its own comparison set: the revising university plus universities that had no threshold revision of their own in the same window. We estimate one specification separately for five outcomes: licenses and options executed, a filing-margin measure, new patent applications, patents issued, and invention disclosures. The comparison that matters is the post-revision difference between upward and downward revisions, each measured against its own controls.

Licensing shows the clearest pattern. After upward revisions, licensing rises relative to clean controls; after downward revisions, it falls. We find no detectable difference before the revision, and the result holds under a stricter post-revision support rule and when we drop events one at a time. Filing and new patent applications move in the same direction, but less precisely. Patents issued are weaker still, and invention disclosures show no matching increase. So the divergence shows up downstream, at licensing, not at the point where faculty first bring inventions into the formal disclosure process.

We do not read the licensing result as evidence that a policy's communicative stance itself causes commercialization. PCSI is a property of the policy text; we do not observe whether faculty read the revised policy, how they perceive it, or how TTO practice changed alongside it. Universities also choose which way to revise; the direction is not assigned. Upward revisions start from predecessor documents with much lower stance scores, which raises a real concern about selection and mean reversion. Direction-label permutation inference helps in a design with so few upward events, but it rests on an exchangeability assumption that the design itself does not guarantee. And once we adjust for testing five outcomes, the statistical evidence for licensing becomes weaker. We therefore treat the estimates as an outcome-specific pattern that follows revisions, not as a causal effect of policy language.

Our main contribution is new evidence on these ordinary rewrites. To our knowledge, this is the first study to treat ordinary within-university IP-policy rewrites as dated events and to trace technology-transfer outcomes around them, from disclosure through licensing. The setting sits between the two kinds of evidence the literature already has: cross-sectional comparisons of individual policy terms, and large national ownership reforms. Ordinary rewrites happen far more often than national reforms, and they are the policy changes universities control themselves. To make them usable as events, we build an auditable workflow that turns incomplete archives of policy documents into a dated sample, keeping the revision universe, the documentary review, and the outcome estimation clearly apart. The design can tell us when change appears and where along the technology-transfer pipeline it shows up. What it cannot do is separate the communicative stance of a rewrite from the organizational changes, on the TTO side or elsewhere, that may come with it.
