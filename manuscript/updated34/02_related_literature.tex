\section{Related literature}\label{sec:lit}

The Bayh--Dole Act let universities and other federal research contractors elect to keep title to qualifying federally funded inventions, subject to federal rights and obligations. Once universities were managing their own inventions, the design of their commercialization rules became an important subject in its own right \citep{MoweryEtAl2001,HendersonJaffeTrajtenberg1998,Sampat2006,GrimaldiEtAl2011}. One line of work asks whether particular policy terms matter. Universities that give inventors a larger royalty share have been found to earn more licensing income \citep{LachSchankerman2008}. Willingness to take equity, and lower royalty shares, go with more startups \citep{DiGregorioShane2003}. Public universities with local-development goals license differently from private ones \citep{BelenzonSchankerman2009}, and European evidence links royalty sharing to inventive effort \citep{ArqueCastellsEtAl2016}.

These studies are useful, but they share a comparability problem. Universities that adopt different contract terms also differ in organizational capacity, research portfolio, governance, and commercialization strategy. When the policy data are corrected and expanded, the royalty-share relationship weakens considerably \citep{OuelletteTutt2020}, which shows how hard it is to treat one contract term as a lever that can be pulled on its own. Some of the cleanest evidence comes instead from reforms that change the rules for everyone in a common setting. \citet{HvideJones2018} show that when Norway moved ownership of university inventions from researchers to institutions, startups and patenting by university researchers fell sharply. \citet{GeunaRossi2011} document the broader European shift toward institutional ownership, and \citet{KenneyPatton2009} argue that different ownership models create different incentives to commercialize. Reforms like these are unusually large, though. They change who holds the rights to an invention, so they act directly on researchers' incentives.

Most changes to university policy are smaller. Institutions rewrite their IP policies from time to time while keeping the basic ownership regime. A rewrite may change disclosure procedures, revenue sharing, conflict-of-interest rules, equity provisions, assignment language, or appeal procedures, along with the way the institution speaks to inventors. Universities and state systems make revisions like these routinely, yet we know of no work that treats them as dated events and links them to later outcomes. We do not know whether commercialization changes after them, or at which stage of the process. That is the gap this paper addresses.

Where a revision might matter depends on how commercialization is organized, and four strands of work bear on that question: on the TTO as an intermediary, on continuing cooperation after licensing, on faculty entry into the formal process, and on what formal rules do inside organizations. A large literature treats the TTO as the place where commercialization performance is decided, with productivity that varies with staffing, incentive pay, routines, and ties to industry \citep{SiegelWaldmanLink2003,SiegelWaldmanAtwater2004,ThursbyKemp2002,MarkmanEtAl2005,OsheaEtAl2005}. \citet{JensenThursbyThursby2003} model the office as an agent of both faculty and the administration, so its own objectives shape disclosure and licensing alike. TTOs sit between science, law, and markets and translate among them, through governance structures, incentive systems, and decision processes of their own \citep{DebackereVeugelers2005,PerkmannEtAl2013,HolgerssonEtAl2019}. Many university inventions are licensed at an early, embryonic stage, so commercialization also depends on inventors, the office, and licensees continuing to work together \citep{JensenThursby2001}. Formal policy is one of the tools that governs those relationships.

This view suggests at least two points in the process where a revision could show up. The first is whether faculty enter the formal process at all. Many potentially commercial inventions are never disclosed \citep{JensenThursbyThursby2003}. Disclosure depends on local norms, on peers, and on how faculty see the TTO \citep{OwenSmithPowell2001,BercovitzFeldman2008}, and part of the historical growth in university licensing has been traced to faculty becoming more willing to disclose \citep{ThursbyThursby2002}. If a revision changes how faculty see their disclosure or ownership obligations, disclosures are a natural place to look for an early response. The second point comes after disclosure, when the office evaluates an invention, decides whether to seek protection, markets the technology, and negotiates with firms. A revision tied to changes in that downstream work could show up in patenting or licensing without any rise in disclosures. Measures for each stage therefore help locate where a post-revision pattern is strongest. The ratio of new patent applications to disclosures, for example, is meant to capture the decision to protect what has been disclosed \citep{ChoudhryPonzio2020,Hall2021}. Our institution-year filing margin is a log-difference version of that ratio, not an invention-level conversion probability.

Theory does not say clearly whether written policy matters at all. Institutional theory allows formal structures to be ceremonial: adopted for legitimacy and partly decoupled from practice \citep{MeyerRowan1977}. Work on administrative burden points the other way, showing how the learning, compliance, and psychological costs built into formal procedures can shape how people deal with institutions \citep{MoynihanHerdHarvey2015,HerdMoynihan2018}. This tension is why we do not claim that wording mechanically causes licensing. A rewrite may also be the visible record of changes that never appear in the text, such as new leadership, a reorganized TTO, new commercialization priorities, or a new strategy toward industry. A difference in outcomes after a revision cannot, by itself, tell a textual channel apart from organizational changes that come with the rewrite.

The companion paper provides a consistent measure of policy communication by applying text-as-data methods to institutional IP documents \citep{SharmaPCSI2026,LoughranMcDonald2011,GentzkowKellyTaddy2019,GrimmerRobertsStewart2022}. We use that measure to classify the direction of each revision and link dated revisions to technology-transfer outcomes. Compared with cross-institution studies, the design compares universities around changes within the same institution, against contemporaneous universities that did not revise. Compared with large ownership reforms, it studies the ordinary revisions that universities choose for themselves. And compared with the TTO literature, having several outcomes lets us ask whether the clearest post-revision pattern appears at disclosure, at patenting, or at licensing. What the paper offers is systematic evidence on when ordinary rewrites happen and where outcomes diverge afterward, not a causal estimate for particular words or clauses.
