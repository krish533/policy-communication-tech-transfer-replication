\section{Introduction}

What happens when a university rewrites its intellectual-property policy? Every research university has one. It says who owns an invention, what faculty must disclose and when, how licensing income is shared, how conflicts of interest are handled, and what inventors can expect from the technology-transfer office (TTO). Universities do not leave these documents alone forever. Every so often, usually after many years, they rewrite them in response to new law, new leadership, or lessons learned in running commercialization.

We already know a good deal about other parts of university commercialization. Studies have looked at who owns university inventions \citep{HvideJones2018,KenneyPatton2009}, at how royalty sharing affects inventors \citep{LachSchankerman2008,ArqueCastellsEtAl2016,OuelletteTutt2020}, and at how TTO staffing, routines, and ties to industry shape licensing and startups \citep{SiegelWaldmanLink2003,MarkmanEtAl2005,DebackereVeugelers2005}. A second line of work points to things that are harder to see. Faculty decide whether to bring an invention forward partly on the basis of how they view the TTO and what their colleagues do \citep{OwenSmithPowell2001,BercovitzFeldman2008}. The TTO sits between faculty, the administration, and firms, and it has goals of its own \citep{JensenThursbyThursby2003}. And because many university inventions are licensed at an early stage, a deal usually needs inventors, the office, and the licensee to keep working together after it is signed \citep{JensenThursby2001}.

Much of this relationship is written down in the IP policy, yet the policy itself has been hard to study. Ownership rules and royalty shares can be compared across universities fairly directly. How a university presents the relationship to its inventors cannot. Two policies can contain similar rules and still read very differently, one as a list of obligations backed by institutional authority, the other as guidance and an offer of help. The companion paper \citep{SharmaPCSI2026} built a way to measure this. Its Policy Communication Stance Index (PCSI) scores the text of each policy by whether it presents rights, obligations, authority, and assistance in supportive or restrictive terms. PCSI describes the text only. It does not measure how generous the rules are, whether faculty like them, or how well the TTO works.

Why would a policy's stance have anything to do with commercialization? There are three plausible routes. Inventors may read the policy and learn from it what they must do and what help they can expect, and written procedures can impose real learning and compliance costs \citep{MoynihanHerdHarvey2015}. TTO staff may carry the policy into the forms, guidance, and routines they use with inventors \citep{SiegelWaldmanLink2003,DebackereVeugelers2005,MarkmanEtAl2005}. Or the rewrite may simply be the visible sign of a wider change, such as a new TTO director or a new commercialization strategy, since formal rules can drift away from what organizations actually do \citep{MeyerRowan1977}. We cannot separate these routes, but they point to different places to look. If a policy works mainly through faculty, disclosures should change. If it works through the office's work after an invention is disclosed, licensing should.

To study rewrites, we built a dataset of dated policy-revision events. We started from the 518 policy documents in the companion paper, covering 150 U.S. research universities from 1944 to 2025, and found every pair of consecutive documents at the same university whose PCSI score changed by more than 0.03, about half a standard deviation. Among universities that report to AUTM, there are 126 such rewrites. We dropped rewrites that overlapped with another rewrite or had no outcome data around them, and then read the old and new documents for the 62 that remained. We kept a rewrite only if the two documents were comparable and the change could be dated with confidence. That leaves 34 rewrites at 32 universities: eight that made the policy more supportive, which we call upward, and 26 that made it more restrictive, which we call downward. No step in this process looked at outcomes.

We then follow technology-transfer outcomes from the AUTM survey around each rewrite: invention disclosures, new patent applications, patents issued, patent filing relative to disclosures, and licenses. Each revising university is compared with universities of similar size and type that did not revise in the same years \citep{CengizEtAl2019,BakerLarckerWang2022}.

Licensing is where the pattern shows up. After upward rewrites, licensing rises relative to universities that did not revise; after downward rewrites, it falls. The gap between the two averages about 0.5 log points over the six years after a rewrite. There is no detectable gap beforehand, and the result holds in a stricter sample and when rewrites are dropped one at a time. Patent applications move the same way but less precisely, patents issued move less, and disclosures show no increase. Whatever changes after a rewrite shows up in the office's work after disclosure, and not in whether faculty bring inventions forward in the first place.

We do not read this as proof that friendlier wording causes more licensing. Universities choose when and how to rewrite. Upward rewrites start from policies with much lower stance scores, so selection and mean reversion are real concerns, and the licensing result weakens once we account for testing five outcomes. What we have is a pattern around rewrite episodes that calls for an explanation.

Our main contribution is evidence on these ordinary rewrites. To our knowledge, this is the first study to treat ordinary within-university IP-policy rewrites as dated events and to follow technology-transfer outcomes around them. Earlier work compares policy terms across universities or studies large national changes in who owns inventions. The rewrites studied here are smaller and far more common, and universities choose them for themselves. The dataset of documented, dated rewrites that makes this possible is part of that contribution. It can be linked to other outcomes, and to new data on what TTOs actually do, to find out which of the three routes is at work.
